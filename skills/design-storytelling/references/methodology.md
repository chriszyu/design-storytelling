# Design Storytelling methodology

Design Storytelling helps designers turn design knowledge into communication that people with different responsibilities can understand and act on. The public foundation is Chris Yu's [Storytelling for UX Designers](https://www.chriszyu.com/writing/storytelling-for-ux-designers).

## Five-move core

### Frame the room

Identify the real audience before selecting content. Discover:

- what they are responsible for;
- what they know, believe, fear, or disagree about;
- which constraints and incentives shape their view;
- whether the job is to inform, discuss, recommend, decide, recruit, teach, or rehearse;
- what should be different after the communication;
- who owns the decision and what evidence they need.

A function can suggest useful questions, but never treat it as a personality type. Product may care about adoption, Engineering about reliability, Support about service load, Legal about auditability, Sales about buyer objections, and leadership about growth, cost, risk, or leverage. Ask what matters in this situation.

### Lead with the point

Make the audience understand what matters as early as the situation allows. Recommendations usually lead with the answer and decision needed. Status updates lead with the headline or exception. Portfolio stories establish the point and stakes early. Research or reflective stories may need enough context or tension to make the conclusion intelligible.

A useful point often contains the conclusion, consequence, scope, and current ask. Supporting detail earns its place by helping the audience understand or trust that point.

### Build the causal spine

Use `Situation -> Tension -> Insight -> Decision -> Change` to find missing logic:

- **Situation:** minimum context about the system, user, goal, and constraints.
- **Tension:** the consequential gap, conflict, risk, opportunity, or unresolved question.
- **Insight:** the evidence-grounded explanation that changes understanding.
- **Decision:** the choice, recommendation, or deliberate non-choice and its rationale.
- **Change:** the observed outcome, current signal, learning, or explicitly uncertain expected effect.

This is a diagnostic, not a template. A proposal may end with expected change and a test. A status update may begin with an exception. A research readout may contain several insights. A retrospective may begin with the result.

### Show judgment and proof

Make the reasoning inspectable:

- what the designer drove, what the team decided, and what was outside their control;
- what was observed or measured;
- what the evidence appears to mean;
- which credible alternatives were considered;
- what each option improves, costs, delays, risks, or prevents;
- how strongly the evidence supports the claim and what could change it.

Calibrated confidence replaces performative certainty. Separate observations from interpretations, forecasts, and opinions. An output can be valuable, but it is not automatically an outcome.

### Land the action

End with what the audience should understand, decide, do, or remember. For a decision, name the recommendation, owner, next step, timing, success or stop signal, and any objection likely to change the choice. For a portfolio or retrospective, the landing may be a supported outcome and an honest learning.

## Responsible business translation

Use:

`User/design evidence -> behavior or capability -> product/operational consequence -> legitimate business relevance -> decision`

Example:

> Several observed administrators could not tell whether an import had failed **(design evidence)**. They waited, retried, or contacted support **(behavior)**, which could slow onboarding and create avoidable service work **(product and operational consequence)**. Activation, reliability, and support burden are therefore relevant, although production impact is not yet proven **(business relevance and limitation)**. Test visible status and a safe recovery path before funding a full rebuild **(decision)**.

Rules:

1. Name the mechanism between each link.
2. Preserve segment, channel, time period, and study conditions.
3. Separate observed effects from expected ones.
4. Distinguish direct measures from proxies.
5. State attribution limits and shared ownership.
6. Match precision to the evidence.
7. Say what would verify or disprove the claim.
8. Keep the user visible.

## Layer the communication

The core story should stand on its own. Put methods, full data, technical detail, alternatives, risk analysis, and implementation material in an organized appendix or supporting layer when they are needed for trust or action. Prepare likely Q&A without scripting away legitimate disagreement. After a decision, record what was decided, accepted tradeoffs, owners, dates, and open evidence requests.

## Plain language and access

Prefer familiar words, concrete verbs, and short sentences when they preserve precision. Explain necessary specialized terms. Remove jargon that is imprecise or ornamental. Simplify the communication, not the truth.

Use readable contrast, semantic structure, text alternatives, captions, and non-color cues where the output format supports them. Protect confidential research, strategy, customer, and personal information.

## Intellectual influences

The methodology is Chris Yu's synthesis. Important external influences include audience transformation and contrast from [Nancy Duarte](https://www.duarte.com/resources/guides-tools/resonate-ebook/), answer-first hierarchy from [Barbara Minto](https://www.barbaraminto.com/), research communication from [Janna Bray](https://jannabray.medium.com/communicating-research-for-impact-99b4794daa7e), decision-meeting discipline from [McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/McKinsey/Business%20Functions/Organization/Our%20Insights/Want%20a%20better%20decision%20Plan%20a%20better%20meeting/Want-a-better-decision-Plan-a-better-meeting.pdf), uncertainty guidance from the [UK Professional Head of Intelligence Assessment](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/explaining-uncertainty-in-uk-intelligence-assessment/explaining-uncertainty-in-uk-intelligence-assessment), and accessibility principles from [W3C WAI](https://www.w3.org/WAI/fundamentals/accessibility-principles/). These ideas are paraphrased rather than reproduced as branded frameworks.
